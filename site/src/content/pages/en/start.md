## 1. What inflation is

Inflation means that prices in general are going up. Not one price: all the things a household buys, taken together.

To measure it, statisticians follow the prices of a **basket**: a long list of what households really buy, from bread and rent to bus tickets and haircuts. If the basket cost €100 a year ago and costs €105 now, prices rose by 5% in a year.

## 2. Why it isn't the same for everyone

The official figure uses one basket for the whole country. But every household's basket is different. A pensioner living alone, a family renting a flat and a well-off couple who travel a lot spend their money in very different ways.

So when some prices jump more than others, some households feel it more. If food and energy get much dearer, the households that spend a bigger part of their money on them are hit harder. The two baskets further down show how differently the poorest and the richest households spend.

## 3. Who the groups are

- **By income, in five "fifths".** Line everyone up from the lowest income to the highest and cut the line into five equal parts. The first part is the poorest fifth, the last the richest fifth. Income here is what a household takes home after tax, adjusted for how many people live on it. Explore shows the limits in euros for a household like yours.
- **By household type:** people living alone, couples, families with children, larger households. Coming soon.
- **Renters, owners and pensioners:** households that pay rent, households that own their home, and households that live mainly on a pension.

## 4. How to read our charts

- **"5% a year"** means the basket costs 5% more than in the same month a year before: €100 of shopping then costs €105 now.
- **The shaded band** around a line: the figures come from a survey of a few thousand households, so they are estimates. The true value is very likely inside the band.
- **The gap** is the poorest fifth's figure minus the richest fifth's. Above zero, prices rose faster for the poorest; below zero, for the richest. It is measured in percentage points: 5% against 3% is a gap of 2 points.

## 5. Where the data comes from

Prices come from ELSTAT, the Greek statistics office, and from Eurostat. What each group buys comes from ELSTAT's household budget survey, which asks thousands of households every year what they spend. We combine the two. Every number can be traced back to its source, and our method and code are open for anyone to check. The method page explains each choice we made, with its sources.
