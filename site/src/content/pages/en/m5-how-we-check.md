## Two published numbers, one rule

Each check takes two figures the same office publishes, where one should follow from the other: a rate and the index it comes from, or an index and the items it is built from. We compute one from the other and compare them period by period. Every period is kept, not only a pass or a fail, and every check says how often the two agree.

## What “agree” means

- **A rate against its own index:** equal to within a billionth of a percentage point. Both come from the same published numbers, so any larger gap means one of them changed.
- **An index rebuilt from its items:** equal once rounded to one decimal, as the office publishes it. The items and their weights are themselves published rounded, so a rebuilt rate can land 0.1 away. We call that rounding.
- **Household types:** each type’s share of households within one percentage point. That limit is our choice, wide enough for the people the two rules can place either way.

## How a difference is worded

A difference reads “these two published numbers are inconsistent”, never “the office made an error”, until its cause is known. Ordinary causes such as rounding, a change of base year or two series joined are published as carefully as unusual ones. A cause is written only once the evidence is saved and cited; until then the difference reads “reason not known yet”.

## What we don’t claim

We don’t say which of two inconsistent numbers is right unless the evidence shows it. We check figures against the office’s own published inputs, not against what we think they should be. We don’t contact the office before publishing a finding.
