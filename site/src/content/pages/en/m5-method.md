## Two published numbers, one rule

Each check takes two figures the same office publishes, where one should follow from the other: a rate and the index it comes from, an index and the items it is built from, a survey's published table and the survey's own file. We compute one from the other and compare them period by period. Every period is kept, the ones that agree as much as the ones that don't, and every check says how often the two agree and over which years.

## What “agree” means

- **A rate against its own index:** equal to within a billionth of a percentage point. Both come from the same published numbers, so any larger discrepancy means one of them changed.
- **An index rebuilt from its items:** equal once rounded to one decimal, as the office publishes it. The items and their weights are themselves published rounded, so a rebuilt rate can land 0.1 away. That is rounding, and a check whose only differences are rounding counts as consistent, with a note saying how many.
- **A survey's published table against its file:** each item's average spending within a cent, the table's own rounding. Items the public file leaves out or cuts are counted apart, not compared.
- **Household types:** each type's share of households within one percentage point, wide enough for the people the two rules can place either way.

## What a check tells you

When two figures agree, the published figure follows from its own inputs: anyone can rebuild it, as we did. When they differ, the check shows where, when and by how much. The cause comes from evidence: a rounding rule, a change of base year, a document that records a revision. Where the evidence names the cause, the finding says so and cites it. Where it doesn't yet, the finding says what we looked at, what we found, and what is still open, and it stays open until the evidence settles it.
